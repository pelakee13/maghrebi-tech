## Introduction: the list you already keep in your head

If you run a salon, a shop, a training centre or a trading desk, you already have a mental list of tasks you do by hand every day. Someone asks the same question in chat and you type the same answer. You copy yesterday's numbers into a report. You re-enter an order that arrived while you were away from the phone.

You know these tasks cost something. What is harder is knowing which one to automate first — and whether automating any of them is even worth it before the next busy season.

This article gives you a scoring method to answer exactly that. It needs no software and no expert. It works on your own tasks, and the output is a short list of what to automate first and what to leave alone for now.

## What "manual work" really costs

A task done by hand has three costs, and most of us only see the first one.

**Time.** This is the obvious one: the minutes spent each day or week on the task itself.

**Attention.** Switching to a repetitive task breaks focus on the work you actually care about. Every interruption has a start and an end; the in-between is lost.

**Error.** A number typed wrong, a booking lost between messages, a report copied from the wrong file. Errors do not always show up on the day they happen.

When you compare tasks on all three, the ranking is often different from what you'd expect.

## The scoring method

For each task you do by hand, score it three ways. Keep the numbers simple — this is a tool to decide, not an accounting system.

**1. How often does it repeat?**

- Once a month or less → 1
- Weekly → 2
- Daily → 3
- Many times a day → 4

**2. How much time does one instance take?**

- A minute or two → 1
- 5–10 minutes → 2
- 15–30 minutes → 3
- More than 30 minutes → 4

**3. What does an error cost?**

- Nothing — it's easily caught and fixed → 1
- A little time or a small amount of money → 2
- A customer waiting, a lost order, or real money → 3
- A broken relationship with a customer, a legal problem, or a major financial loss → 4

Multiply the three numbers. The score runs from 1 to 64.

- **40 and above:** automate this first. The pain compounds every single day.
- **20–39:** worth automating soon. Look at it after the first one is done.
- **10–19:** a candidate, but not urgent.
- **Below 10:** leave it. The effort of automating may cost more than the task.

## The four usual suspects

In most small businesses, the same four tasks score highest. Here's how to apply the method to each.

### Orders and reservations

Score the full cycle: receiving the order, confirming it, recording it, and answering "where is my order?" later. Frequency is usually 4 (many times a day), time is 2–3 per instance, and error cost is 3 (a lost order is a real problem). Total: high.

### Repeated customer questions

"How much is it?", "When are you open?", "Do you deliver?". The time per instance looks tiny (1–2 points), but frequency is 4 and you get interrupted constantly. Error cost is usually low. The score sits in the middle — which often means it is a good first project precisely because it's simple and hard to get wrong.

### Daily and weekly reports

Where do the numbers come from? If you collect them from chats and notebooks and type them into one place, score the time honestly. Reports often take 30 minutes or more per day (3–4), and a wrong number is expensive (3–4). Frequency is high. This is often the single highest-scoring task in the method.

### Booking and scheduling

A salon or clinic with phone or chat bookings does the same thing over and over: take the request, check availability, confirm, remind. Frequency is high, the error cost of a double-booking is real (3), and the time per instance is medium. A strong candidate.

## What to do with the scores

Write down every task on one page. Score each. Sort them. The top one is your answer.

A few things to keep in mind:

- **Start with the highest score, not the easiest task.** A simple task is tempting but may not save you much.
- **Automate one process first, not all of them.** Measure for a week or two before moving to the next.
- **Some tasks should stay manual.** Anything that needs judgement, negotiation or a human conversation should stay in human hands. The method scores the repetitive core — not the parts that make your business personal.

## When the method says "not yet"

If every task scores below 20, automation is not your first problem. Look at the process itself: maybe the task repeats because the way you work is not written down anywhere, or because data lives in too many places. Cleaning that up is worth more than any bot.

## Conclusion: one score, one first step

You don't need to automate the whole business. Score the tasks you already know are repetitive. Pick the highest one. Do that first.
